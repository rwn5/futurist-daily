Flock Pitched a Plan To Turn Uber and Lyft Drivers Into Roaming Surveillance Vehicles

Surveillance company Flock Safety drafted a plan to enlist ~350,000 rideshare and delivery drivers as mobile license plate scanners feeding its law enforcement network. The pitch reframes an everyday gig worker as an involuntary node in a mass surveillance grid — without passengers or drivers knowing.

SAP has frozen most hiring and travel budgets to fund its accelerating AI spend. It's a clean signal: legacy enterprise software companies are cannibalizing their own operations to stay competitive in the AI race.

Commentary & Synthesis

The Thread

Two stories this week pull in opposite directions — and together they tell you everything about where we are. SAP is gutting headcount and travel to fund AI infrastructure, while Anthropic ships autonomous coding agents as the default experience. The enterprise world is liquidating its human overhead in real time to pay for the AI transition. This isn't a future trend; it's this quarter's P&L decision.

"The agent management layer is becoming its own skill set — the most valuable job of 2026 might be knowing how to supervise the machines doing all the other jobs."
The surveillance story deserves more attention than it's getting. Flock Safety's pitch to instrument 350,000 Uber and Lyft drivers as roaming license plate collectors is a glimpse at how private surveillance infrastructure scales without legislation — by simply plugging into existing gig economy labor. Meanwhile, a Kansas town shuts down in-person democracy rather than deal with the backlash from approving a data center. The physical footprint of the AI build-out is generating civic conflict that tech coverage mostly ignores.

Ethan Mollick invoking Vinge's soft takeoff isn't abstract philosophy right now. When Anthropic defaults to autonomous agents, OpenAI accidentally attacks Hugging Face during a training run, and a startup raises money to build self-improving AI, the pace of change has already outrun the governance conversation. The question isn't whether we're in a takeoff — it's whether anyone is watching the instruments.
