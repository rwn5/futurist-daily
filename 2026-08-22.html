Commentary & Synthesis

The Thread

Two stories that look unrelated are actually the same story. Amazon destroying rare books for training data and the "subtlefakes" nonconsensual image wave on X are both downstream of the same dynamic: the inputs to AI systems carry almost no cost when someone else owns the thing being consumed. Books, bodies, rare archives — the training pipeline has no natural stopping point unless external friction is applied.

"The training data grab has no natural stopping point unless external friction is applied."
The friction is starting to come from unexpected places. A township using electrical infrastructure moratoriums to block a nuclear weapons data center is not a story about NIMBYism — it's a preview of how communities will resist the physical footprint of AI buildout when federal and corporate channels fail them. Zoning boards and municipal councils are becoming unlikely AI governance actors.

On the model side, Gemini 3.7 Flash scoring 84.6% on ARC-AGI-2 at a quarter per task is the quiet headline of the week. The benchmark that was supposed to resist near-term AI is being picked apart by a cost-optimized model, not a frontier one. Meanwhile the agent security hole — inherited credentials, no sandboxing — is sitting wide open while every major company races to ship agent workflows. The a16z security content this week isn't thought leadership, it's triage.

Ethan Mollick's prediction about public AI hypocrisy is the frame for all of it. Regulatory pressure will build on AI in the abstract while adoption accelerates in the specific. That gap is where the next five years of tech policy gets made — and mostly fumbled.
