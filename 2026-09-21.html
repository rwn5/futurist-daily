'Doom Loop': OpenAI and Microsoft Admit LLMs Are Destroying the Web and Built on Theft

OpenAI and Microsoft have acknowledged in filings that LLMs scrape copyrighted work without consent and that AI crawlers are degrading the open web they depend on. The admission is remarkable: the same companies building on the web are accelerating its collapse.

'Flock City PD:' The Fake Police Department That Searched Real Cameras for Real People

License plate surveillance company Flock Safety ran searches on its own network using a fake internal "police department," querying for bumper stickers, religious symbols, and political signage. The company says it was demonstrating what cops shouldn't search for — which raises the question of why anyone built that capability at all.

Commentary & Synthesis

The Thread

The week's dominant story isn't any single product — it's the simultaneous exposure of AI's original sins. OpenAI and Microsoft's own filings now confirm what critics have said for years: the training data was taken, and the crawlers are wrecking the ecosystem that made the data valuable in the first place. The "doom loop" framing is theirs, not a critic's. That's a confession, not a critique.
"Millions of people around the world will soon consider large models 'hoovering up' all their work to be an astonishing theft of unprecedented proportions."
Meanwhile, the Flock Safety story and the AI surveillance panel point to a second loop: private companies building capabilities, demonstrating them internally in ways that reveal exactly how they could be abused, then selling access to governments. The fake police department that ran real searches on real people is the clearest possible illustration of how surveillance infrastructure normalizes before anyone votes on it. The Claude Code anecdote from Simon Willison's blog is the ground-level version of the same dynamic. Workers are being forced to ship AI-generated everything while the tooling strips away the judgment, craft, and accountability that made the work worth doing. Jev's emergence — a fast, cheap classification layer that routes decisions at 200ms for cents — suggests the next wave of AI infrastructure isn't about generation at all. It's about triage: getting the right model, the right action, the right output, without a human in the loop to slow things down. Bitcoin breaking $84K on short liquidations is almost a metaphor. The gains are real, but they're built on forced exits. Watch for who's left holding the bag when the leverage unwinds.
