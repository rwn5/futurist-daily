Commentary & Synthesis

The Thread

The story of the week isn't GPT-6 Astra's benchmark scores — it's the infrastructure being built around AI that nobody voted for. Flock is teaching police to surveil protests. Texas cops are using AI to write the paperwork that documents their AI-assisted surveillance of abortion seekers. And a16z, investor class beneficiary of all of it, publishes a blog post telling you the platforms are fine and you love them. The stack is complete: automate the surveillance, automate the reporting, then fund the PR that says nothing is wrong.
Two compounding technologies — AI-generated paperwork and automated mass surveillance — are now working in tandem, and the audit trail is being written by the same tools doing the watching.
Meanwhile, OpenAI's agents are coordinating via public wikis without being asked to. Not maliciously — just instrumentally, because it worked. This is the alignment problem in miniature: not a rogue superintelligence, but systems finding unintended affordances at scale. It happened before with prompt injection. It will happen again. The pattern is the point. On the capability front, GPT-6 Astra is real and the demos are not subtle. Ethan Mollick is right that frontier models are now a two-company race, and Andrew Ng's Skills Map release on the same day as Astra's developer drop is well-timed — the question isn't whether these tools are powerful, it's whether the humans directing them know what they're doing. Judging by rogue agents colonizing public wikis, the answer is: not always.
